Debt Accounting for Small Businesses

May 20, 2024 • 7 min read • By Detally Team

You run a small coffee shop, a retail store, or a freelance business. Every day, customers ask to buy on credit. Suppliers expect payment within 30 days. By the end of the month, you have no clear picture of who owes you what — and who you owe.

This is where debt accounting becomes essential. Not just for large corporations, but for every business that extends credit or receives it.

Let's break down the fundamentals of small business debt accounting.

What is debt accounting?

Debt accounting is the practice of tracking money owed to your business (accounts receivable) and money your business owes to others (accounts payable). It's a core component of financial operations that directly impacts your cash flow.

In accounting terms:

  • Accounts Receivable (AR): Customers owe you money. This is an asset on your balance sheet.
  • Accounts Payable (AP): You owe money to suppliers or vendors. This is a liability.

Many small business owners focus only on revenue and ignore debt management in both directions. The result? Profits look good on paper, but cash is nowhere to be found.

Why small businesses need proper debt accounting

Without a proper system for debt tracking, you risk:

  • Cash flow problems: You can't pay suppliers or staff if customers don't pay you.
  • Bad debt write-offs: Uncollectible receivables become expenses that eat your profits.
  • Missed payment discounts: Many suppliers offer 2-5% discounts for early payment — you're leaving money on the table.
  • Strained relationships: Unclear debt records lead to disputes with customers and vendors.
  • Tax reporting errors: Your accountant needs accurate AR/AP numbers for proper tax filing.

"Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, but cash is king." — Small business accounting wisdom

The two sides of debt accounting

1. Accounts Receivable (Money owed to you)

When you sell products or services on credit, you create an account receivable. Proper AR management includes:

  • Sending invoices immediately after delivery
  • Setting clear payment terms (Net 15, Net 30, or Due upon receipt)
  • Tracking aging — how long each invoice has been outstanding
  • Sending payment reminders before and after due dates
  • Documenting collection efforts for overdue accounts

A good rule of thumb: If an invoice is over 90 days overdue, it's at high risk of becoming bad debt.

2. Accounts Payable (Money you owe)

When you buy supplies, inventory, or services on credit, you create an account payable. Smart AP management involves:

  • Recording all vendor invoices immediately
  • Knowing payment due dates to avoid late fees
  • Taking advantage of early payment discounts
  • Prioritizing payments based on vendor relationships and interest rates
  • Maintaining good relationships with suppliers through on-time payment

Key debt accounting metrics every owner should track

  • Days Sales Outstanding (DSO): Average days to collect payment from customers. Lower is better. Calculate: (Accounts Receivable ÷ Total Credit Sales) × Days in Period
  • Days Payable Outstanding (DPO): Average days to pay suppliers. Higher is better (within terms). Calculate: (Accounts Payable ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × Days in Period
  • Bad Debt Ratio: Percentage of sales that become uncollectible. Calculate: (Bad Debt Write-offs ÷ Total Credit Sales) × 100
  • Aging Report: Breaks down AR by how old it is (0-30 days, 31-60 days, 61-90 days, 90+ days). Any significant amount over 90 days is a red flag.

How to set up a debt ledger for your small business

You don't need expensive accounting software to start. A simple debt ledger should include:

  • Date of transaction
  • Customer/vendor name
  • Invoice number (if any)
  • Amount owed
  • Due date
  • Status (pending / partial / paid / overdue)
  • Date paid (when settled)

This can be a spreadsheet, a notebook, or — better yet — a dedicated debt tracking tool like Detally. The key is consistency: every transaction gets recorded immediately.

Common debt accounting mistakes (and how to avoid them)

  • Mistake #1: Not recording small debts. "It's only $20, I'll remember." No, you won't. Every dollar counts. Record everything.
  • Mistake #2: Mixing personal and business debt. Your coffee shop's AR and your personal loans to friends should never be in the same ledger. Keep them separate.
  • Mistake #3: No aging analysis. If you don't know how old your receivables are, you don't know which ones are at risk. Review your aging report weekly.
  • Mistake #4: Being afraid to follow up. Many small business owners avoid collection calls because they're uncomfortable. Professional, polite follow-ups are normal business practice.
  • Mistake #5: No backup for critical data. If your paper ledger gets lost or your spreadsheet gets corrupted, you have no record. Use a tool with automatic backup — or keep a duplicate.
